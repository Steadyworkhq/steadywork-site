How to automate repetitive tasks at work

Automation does not have to mean software projects. Most repeated office tasks can be made smaller with what you already have, and a few are worth handing over completely.

Checked 2026-11-18. Menus in other products change, so a label may differ slightly on your screen.

Short answer: list the tasks you repeat, pick the one that is frequent and follows rules, and use the simplest option that works: a template first, then a feature built into the software you already use, then a tool that does the task for you.

Step 1: decide what is worth it

A task is a good candidate when it is frequent, follows the same steps, and uses information that is written down. Count the time honestly: fifteen minutes a day is more than an hour a week.

A task is a poor candidate when every case is different, when it relies on what someone remembers, or when it happens twice a year.

Step 2: try the simplest thing first

  1. Templates. A saved email, a document with the blanks marked, a spreadsheet with the formulas already in. No software to learn, and often half the time saved.
  2. What your software already does. Most business tools have a few automatic features that nobody switched on: recurring invoices and payment reminders in accounts software, rules and scheduled sending in email, alerts in Smartsheet. See our guides on QuickBooks reminders, Outlook follow-ups and Smartsheet emails.
  3. Connecting two tools. Services such as Zapier and Power Automate pass information from one app to another when something happens. They are capable, and they ask you to think like a builder. See Is Zapier hard to use?
  4. A tool that does the task. Newer AI tools take a description of the chore and do it, handing you the result to check.

Step 3: write the task down properly

Whatever you use, it will only be as good as the instructions. Write down:

  • What starts it. A day of the week, something arriving, or you deciding it is time.
  • Which items count. "Invoices more than seven days overdue", not "late invoices".
  • The exceptions. Who is left out, and when not to do it at all.
  • What done looks like. One email per customer, with the invoice attached, from your address.
  • What you want to be told. What was skipped and why, and when there was nothing to do.

People usually get the first two and forget the last three. The exceptions are where automations go wrong.

Step 4: keep control

  • Start with it preparing the work and you approving it.
  • Check its first few weeks closely, then spot-check.
  • Make sure you can pause it with one click.
  • Know where to look to see what it did.
